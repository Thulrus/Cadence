5 Seed-Starting Mistakes (And How to Fix Them)

Mistake number one: starting too early.

It’s so tempting. It’s cold, you’re stuck inside, and you want to see something green. But if you start your tomatoes in January, by the time it’s warm enough to plant them outside, they’ll be leggy, root-bound, and stressed out.

Here’s the rule I follow. Look up your average last frost date, then count backward. Tomatoes and peppers want about six to eight weeks. Brassicas like broccoli and cabbage need four to six. Cucumbers and squash only need two to three, because they grow fast and hate having their roots disturbed.

Mistake number two: not enough light.

A sunny windowsill sounds great in theory, but in the middle of winter, it just isn’t enough. The days are short, the sun is low, and your seedlings will stretch toward the glass like they’re trying to escape. That stretching is called etiolation, and it gives you weak, pale, floppy stems.

The fix is simple: get a cheap LED shop light, hang it two to three inches above the seedlings, and run it for fourteen to sixteen hours a day. As the plants grow, raise the light. Don’t raise the plants. You’ll be amazed at how much sturdier everything looks within a week.

[PAUSE]

Mistake number three: overwatering.

This is the big one. More seedlings die from too much love than from neglect. Constantly soggy soil starves roots of oxygen and invites a fungus called damping-off, which makes a seedling pinch at the base and fall over overnight. It’s heartbreaking.

Instead, water from the bottom. Set your tray in a shallow pan of water, let the soil soak it up for about twenty minutes, then drain it. Before you water again, stick a finger in the soil. If the top half inch is still damp, wait. A little airflow from a small fan helps a ton, too.

Mistake number four: using garden soil.

I know, I know. You have a whole yard full of dirt. But garden soil is too heavy for tiny roots, it compacts in small containers, and it can carry pests and diseases you really don’t want indoors.

Buy a bag of seed-starting mix. It’s light, fluffy, and sterile, and it’s usually made from peat or coconut coir, plus perlite or vermiculite. It’s about eight to ten dollars a bag, and it’ll fill dozens of cells. Totally worth it.

Mistake number five: skipping the hardening-off step.

Your seedlings have lived their whole lives in a gentle, climate-controlled paradise. If you move them straight into full sun and wind, they’ll get scorched. I’ve lost entire trays of peppers in a single afternoon this way.

So, over seven to ten days, ease them into it. Start with one hour in the shade on day one. Add an hour or two each day, then slowly introduce direct sun. Bring them in if it dips below forty-five degrees at night. By the end of the week, they’ll be tough enough to handle the real world.

[PAUSE]

Quick recap.

Don’t start too early. Give them real light. Water from the bottom, and only when they need it. Use proper seed-starting mix. And harden them off before transplanting.

If you get those five things right, you’re already ahead of most gardeners. Honestly, it’s the difference between a tray of sad, spindly sprouts and a row of stocky, deep-green plants that are ready to take off.
