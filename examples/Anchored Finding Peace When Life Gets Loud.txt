Anchored: Finding Peace When Life Gets Loud

Brothers and sisters, good morning. It’s a joy to be with you today.

[PAUSE]

I’d like to begin with a simple question. When was the last time you felt completely at peace? Not just a quiet moment, but a deep, settled calm that stayed with you through the whole day.

If you’re like me, that answer might take a minute. Life gets loud. Between work, family, news, and the phone in our pockets, peace can feel like something we visit instead of something we live in.

First, an invitation.

In Matthew 11:28, the Savior says, “Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest.” Notice he doesn’t say, “Fix yourself, then come.” He says come as you are, heavy-laden and all.

Second, a shepherd.

Most of us know Psalm 23 by heart, or at least the first part. Verse 1 reads, “The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want.” Then, in verses 4 through 6, David moves through the darkest part of the journey and ends in confidence. He doesn’t say there’s no valley. He says, “Thou art with me.”

I find that comforting, because the promise isn’t a life without trouble. The promise is company in the trouble.

[PAUSE]

Third, trust.

Proverbs 3:5–6 is one of the first passages I memorized as a boy. “Trust in the Lord with all thine heart; and lean not unto thine own understanding. In all thy ways acknowledge him, and he shall direct thy paths.”

I’ve leaned on my own understanding plenty of times, and I can tell you it’s a poor crutch. Looking back over the last 20 years, the best turns in my life were ones I never saw coming.

Fourth, a definition of faith.

Hebrews 11:1 says, “Now faith is the substance of things hoped for, the evidence of things not seen.” Then the whole of chapter 11 is a long line of people who acted before they could see. Noah built a boat on dry ground. Abraham left home without a map. Moses walked into the Red Sea, and Exodus 14, verses 21 and 22, tells us the waters parted only after they had begun to move.

Faith, it seems, is rarely a feeling. It’s usually a step.

[PAUSE]

Fifth, patience.

Sometimes the answer is to wait, and that’s hard. Isaiah 40:31 reads, “But they that wait upon the Lord shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings as eagles; they shall run, and not be weary; and they shall walk, and not faint.”

Notice the order there: soar, run, walk. The hardest of the three is the last. Anyone can be inspired for a moment, but walking and not fainting is what a faithful life looks like on an ordinary Tuesday.

The scriptures are full of waiting. Noah waited 40 days and 40 nights while the rain fell, as recorded in Genesis 7:12. Israel wandered 40 years. And the disciples fished all night in John 21 and caught nothing, until a voice from the shore told them where to cast the net. They hauled in 153 fish. Someone counted. That detail has always made me smile.

Sixth, forgiveness.

Peter once asked how many times he should forgive his brother, and then offered a generous number himself: “till seven times?” In Matthew 18:21–22, the Savior answers, “I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven.”

That’s 490, if you’re keeping score. The point, of course, is to stop keeping score.

[PAUSE]

Finally, courage.

Second Timothy 1:7 says, “For God hath not given us the spirit of fear; but of power, and of love, and of a sound mind.” And 1 John 4:18 adds that perfect love casts out fear. When I’m anxious, those two verses are where I go first.

Maybe you’re carrying something heavy this morning. A diagnosis, a strained relationship, a job that’s gone sideways, or a quiet worry you haven’t told anyone. Psalm 46:10 gives a simple instruction: “Be still, and know that I am God.”

Not “figure it out.” Be still.

I’d like to leave you with a short challenge. This week, pick one of the passages we talked about today, whether it’s Psalm 121:1–2, Philippians 4:6–7, or any other, and read it each morning for seven days. Write down what you notice. I think you’ll find, as I have, that peace is less a place we arrive at and more an anchor we learn to hold.
